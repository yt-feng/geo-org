Eco-GEO: What to do when the opponent is mentioned by AI first: A guide to branding intellectual property services and tool selection for GEO

During periods of increased competition, the fact that competitors appear first in AI responses does not mean they should take over rankings. This article explains how to turn qualifications, processes, and commitments into verifiable brand facts. It also outlines the division of tasks among three tools: technical verification, platform reference observation, and manual question sampling, to reduce the risk of misstatement and false comparisons.

Key Points Summary

  • GEO’s goal is not to have the model vouch for you, but to ensure that when AI answers questions like “Who can provide certain intellectual property services,” it receives clear, consistent, and verifiable brand information. Whether a reference is made or not is determined by the platform’s system, and no guarantees can be made.
  • During periods of increased competition, the order of countermeasures should be “first correcting serious misstatements, then expanding the scope of issues.” The two types of work should be accounted for separately in terms of working hours.
  • The tool is used in three categories: verification of sites and structured data, observation of references on the platform side, and review of self-drawn questions. What the tool produces are observations and clues; it does not replace manual verification or equal performance scores.

Scope of application: This article is aimed at sales managers in intellectual property service agencies, discussing the organization of brand facts at the public information level and the selection of tools. Limitations: Different AI platforms disclose limited information on their citation mechanisms, and these may change over time. The article does not have access to any AI usage rates, baseline errors, or conversion data from industry buyers. The studies cited are based on other experimental settings and cannot be extrapolated to the industry’s effectiveness. The demonstration process in the article is hypothetical and not based on real customer cases.

Question: AI first mentions the opponent; what should be sold in response?

A common scenario: During communication with clients, salespeople find that the client uses an AI assistant for preliminary screening. The responses list several competitors and mention their own agency, but the scope of services is too broad, or “agency applications” and “giving legal opinions” are confused. The first reaction in such cases is often to “find a way to improve our ranking or get more mentions of us from the AI.” This approach deserves rethinking.

The difference between generative retrieval and traditional search is that the system first retrieves pages, filters sources, and then generates quoted answers [S3]. This study analyzed 17,211 quoted instances across 10 platforms and found that quotes were concentrated on sources preferred by each platform. Authors also clearly stated that repeated citation itself does not indicate the credibility of the source [S3]. This suggests a reality: Pages that can be cited are those that “can be captured, understood, and used as evidence,” rather than the intensity of brand self-evaluation.

Key judgment: Translate brand promises into verifiable evidence

For intellectual property institutions, brand promises typically include “professionalism, reliability, and transparent processes.” These words appear a hundred times on the website’s homepage, but they don’t help the model determine their authenticity. What really matters is translating these promises into verifiable facts: professional qualifications and personnel names, scope of services provided, process steps and responsibilities of various parties, pricing standards and potential additional costs, and publicly discloseable case examples.

External statistical data can serve as a unified reference for factual statements. For example, WIPO provides statistical and data services for patents, utility models, trademarks, and designs classification[S2]. If an institution uses terms like “number of applications” or “authorization trends” on its page, it must clarify the data source, year, and scope of the statistics to avoid presenting statistical statements as guarantees of its own results. This is a matter of factual alignment, without any guarantee of effectiveness.

It’s not technically mysterious. According to Google’s instructions, for a page to become a supported link for AI functions, it must be indexable, able to serve as an abstract display, have no additional technical barriers, and ensure that structured data matches the visible text on the page. Important content must also exist in text form[S1]. In other words, if the promises are written in structured tags but there’s no corresponding text, the direction is reversed.

Tool selection section: Each of the three types of tools is used for a specific purpose; do not mix them together.

  1. is a technical verification tool. is used to verify the consistency of indexing, robots settings, structured data, and visible text. Google recommends verifying the site in Search Console to quickly identify technical issues [S1]. These tools answer whether the page can be read correctly.
  2. is a platform-side reference tool for observations. Bing Webmaster Tools’ AI Performance provides metrics such as total citations, cited pages, and grounding queries in the public preview. These numbers do not represent ranking, authority, or position within a single answer. [S4] is suitable for identifying “which pages are used as evidence” and should not be used as a scorecard.
  3. involves manual question review. involves designated personnel posing fixed questions based on actual sales issues (e.g., “How to handle partial rejection of a trademark”). They record the AI’s responses to these questions along with the referenced pages, distinguishing between facts, inferences, and incorrect generalizations. This is the only step that can directly verify whether the “rephrase” is accurate.

The division of labor among the three types of tools should be included in the process: when abnormalities are detected, they are manually determined in nature; technical aspects then correct the pages, followed by re-verification. None of these steps should claim to be “Efficient with GEO” on its own.

Example of breaking down a conditional statement: A complete process for correcting a misstatement

The following is a demonstration method and does not involve real clients. It is assumed that during the question selection process, it was found that an AI response described this institution as being capable of handling all foreign trademark procedures, when in reality its service scope is limited to specific countries and stages.

  • Week 1: Identify the source of facts. Use the reference pages in the quiz records to trace back and check if those pages only list the collaboration networks without clearly defining service boundaries. Also, verify whether the structured data matches the main text.
  • Week 1: Make corrections and keep records. should clearly state the scope of services, matters not covered, and any potential changes to information in plain language; keep records of modifications and dates. Corrections must be based on the requirement of factual consistency, as described by Google regarding the consistency of structured data with visible text [S1].
  • Week 2 to 3: Retest of the same questions. Repeat the same set of questions to check if the original errors still exist in the answers, and observe whether the referenced pages have changed. If using Bing’s observation panel, compare the referenced pages with the grounding query terms, but remember that these are sample data [S4].

It should be noted that changes in references are affected by the crawling and indexing cycle. Google clearly states that crawling may take several days to months[S1]. Therefore, “two-week retest” can only serve as an observation point and does not constitute a commitment to a time when corrections will take effect.

Beware of shortcuts: citation paths published with low barriers and white hat boundaries

The sales manager may be exposed to GEO service quotes involving “rapid scale-up and multi-platform publishing.” The aforementioned study documented a strategy in which content was published in bulk on platforms with lower entry barriers, allowing entry into certain AI search references in the short term. The study described a purchase case costing $14 and stated that this was just a single instance, without estimating the overall market success rate [S3]. For intellectual property institutions, the problem with this approach lies not in technology but in the consequences: once customers discover that the content was written merely to meet citation requirements, the credibility of the professional commitments will be damaged, and such content may also be used by AI as evidence, leading to incorrect dissemination.

The White Hat approach adheres to authenticity, verifiability, and traceability: all claims are marked with sources or authors; no results are promised; uncertain matters are described under conditions. Consultative and analytical materials also indicate that generative optimization relies on signals of authority, credibility, structure, and timeliness, with the focus being on ease of retrieval rather than ranking[S5]. This material is an opinion-based analysis; the growth data mentioned therein cannot be used as industry benchmarks, but the direction of “organizing content for retrieval” can serve as a working principle.

Action list and observation indicators

Actions that can be carried out within two weeks, organized by person responsible:

  • Led by the sales manager, establish a ledger of brand facts: qualifications, personnel signatures, service scope and exclusions, process milestones, pricing standards. Each entry can be verified for source and latest check date.
  • Standardize the presentation on the official website, Q&A pages, and platform introduction, focusing on eliminating multiple ways of presenting the same fact.
  • Designate a signature expert and a reviewer to conduct the baseline questionnaire (it is recommended to cover 10–15 types of questions commonly encountered in sales). Keep the original sentences and referenced pages intact.
  • Prioritize fixing serious misstatements that have been verified and may affect customers' judgment, recording labor hours separately; expand the scope of issues as the second phase.
  • Set clear boundaries with compliance confirmations in public statements, and avoid making commitments that cannot be verified.

Observation metrics should use procedural indicators rather than result commitments: completion rate of fact logs (verified items/total items), severe misstatement rate (number of questions with incorrect summaries/total number of questions), changes in correct repetition rates for the same questions, and whether the distribution of cited pages is concentrated on the pages that should be cited. The total number of citations and the number of inquiries can serve as background observations, but they should not be used as assessment commitments, because both the platform selection mechanism and the level of disclosure are uncontrollable [S4].

Finally, back to the question itself: When the opponent is first mentioned by AI, what truly can change the situation isn’t the ranking of an article. Instead, it’s your qualifications, boundaries, and commitments—things that can be clearly explained, verified, and matched on any page or in any format. This is both the cost of White Hat GEO and its only reliable asset during times of increased competition.
